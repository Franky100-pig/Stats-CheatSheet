Two Categorical Variables: Tables, Graphs & Association

Two-way (contingency) tables, joint / marginal / conditional relative frequencies, side-by-side and segmented bar charts, and the reasoning that turns a difference in conditional distributions into evidence of association — without claiming causation.

1. Two-Way Tables and the Three Relative Frequencies

A two-way table, also called a contingency table, summarises two categorical variables measured on the same individuals. Cell entries may be counts (frequencies) or proportions (relative frequencies). Always ask "out of whom?" before dividing — the denominator is the whole exam trap in this topic.

Joint, Marginal and Conditional Relative Frequency
joint relative frequency = cell / table total
marginal relative frequency = row total / table total  (or column total / table total)
conditional relative frequency = cell / that row's total  (or that column's total)
A marginal distribution describes one variable alone (the row/column totals in the margins). A conditional distribution describes one variable within a single level of the other — the one used to compare groups.

  • Marginal: P(Tutoring) = 60/120 = 0.50; P(Passed) = 72/120 = 0.60.
  • Conditional: P(Passed | Tutoring) = 42/60 = 0.70 versus P(Passed | No tutoring) = 30/60 = 0.50.

2. Graphical Displays and Deciding Whether Variables Are Associated

Side-by-side bar charts, segmented (stacked) bar charts and mosaic plots all display the distribution of one categorical variable at each level of the other. Segmented bars must be scaled to 100% per group, otherwise unequal group sizes make the picture misleading.

Association Rule
conditional distributions differ across groups → the variables are associated
conditional distributions are (nearly) the same → no evidence of association
In the worked table, 70% vs 50% is a clear difference, so tutoring status and passing are associated in these data. State the direction with the numbers, in context.
Association is not causation

An association in an observational two-way table does not justify "tutoring caused the pass rate". Only a randomized experiment that assigns the explanatory variable supports a causal conclusion; otherwise name a plausible confounding variable.

3. Interpretation Templates and Exam Traps

Exam-safe wording
  • "Among students who received tutoring, 42/60 = 70% passed, compared with 30/60 = 50% among those who did not. Because these conditional relative frequencies differ, the data suggest an association between tutoring and passing."
  • "The marginal distribution of passing is 72/120 = 60% passed and 48/120 = 40% failed, ignoring tutoring status."
Traps the exam actually tests
  • Wrong denominator: 42/120 is a joint relative frequency, not P(Passed | Tutoring). Conditional claims divide by the row/column total.
  • Comparing raw counts when group sizes differ — always convert to relative frequencies first.
  • Describing only one group. A comparison needs at least two conditional distributions.
  • "No association" is not proof of independence; say "the data do not show a difference".
  • Association ≠ causation unless the data come from a randomized experiment.
